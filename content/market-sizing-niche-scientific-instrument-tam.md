Summary: Claiming a $15 billion market destroys your credibility with grant reviewers and investors. Learn how to construct a bottom-up market sizing model for a niche scientific instrument that wins non-dilutive grants and guides commercialization.

Every grant evaluation season, reviewers for the EIC Accelerator, NWO Take-off, and national innovation funds read hundreds of proposals that repeat the same fatal cliché: *"The global analytical instrumentation and photonics market is projected to reach $48.5 billion by 2030; capturing just 1% of this market represents a $485 million opportunity."*

When you are commercializing a **niche scientific instrument**—such as an ultra-fast correlator for single-photon microscopy or an in-situ grazing-incidence X-ray scattering cell—this top-down macro figure does not impress evaluators. It immediately signals that the founders do not know who their actual customers are. As we explain in [the market analysis trap of generic TAM]({filename}/market-analysis-trap-generic-tam.md), inflated macro numbers mask the absence of primary customer discovery and obscure the real unit economics of specialized hardware.

## Why Macro Reports Fail the Niche Scientific Instrument

Syndicated market research reports bundle everything from mass-produced hospital blood analyzers and routine QA/QC spectrophotometers to defense-grade satellite optics into a single multi-billion-dollar figure. Your specialized apparatus does not compete with a $4,000 benchtop pH meter, nor can it be sold to 50,000 general chemistry laboratories.

In reality, the Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) for a cutting-edge niche scientific instrument is defined by a highly specific scientific or industrial bottleneck. There may be only 180 university laboratories, synchrotron beamlines, and corporate R&D groups worldwide working on that exact physical regime. Pretending those 180 labs represent a multi-billion-dollar venture market leads to two catastrophic mistakes:

1. **Grant Rejection:** Seasoned technical reviewers know the size of their own research community. If you claim thousands of potential buyers in a field with 60 active Principal Investigators globally, your entire execution plan loses credibility.
2. **Strategic Misallocation:** Believing your own inflated TAM pushes founders into the [startup trap]({filename}/startup_trap.md)—raising high-friction equity to hire a bloated corporate team for a market that naturally wants to buy 25 systems a year.

## How to Build a Bottom-Up Lab-Count Model

A rigorous, defensible market sizing model for [the economics of commercializing a niche scientific instrument]({filename}/economics-commercializing-niche-scientific-instrument.md) is built strictly from the bottom up. Instead of citing consultancy PDFs, you count actual prospective buyers by name, facility, and funding cycle:

$$
\text{Annual SOM} = \left( N_{\text{tier-1}} \times P_{\text{adopt}} \times \text{ASP} \right) + \left( N_{\text{installed}} \times \text{ARR}_{\text{service}} \right)
$$

To populate this model for an [anatomy of a winning deep tech grant]({filename}/anatomy-of-a-winning-deep-tech-grant.md) or TTO commercialization dossier, use four concrete data sources:

* **Bibliometric and Citation Mapping:** Query Web of Science or Scopus for papers published in the last 36 months that utilize the specific experimental technique your instrument automates or improves. Extract the unique institutional affiliations and corresponding authors. If 140 distinct research groups published using makeshift home-built setups, those 140 PIs are your primary TAM.
* **Public Grant Databases (CORDIS, NIH RePORTER, NWO):** Search active grant awards over €500k in your domain. A lab that was awarded a €2.5M ERC Consolidator grant six months ago has both the scientific mandate and the unspent equipment budget required to buy Serial #001 through #005.
* **Core Facility and Synchrotron Registries:** Map national nanofabrication centers, Euro-BioImaging nodes, or beamline end-stations where your tool upgrades shared user infrastructure.
* **Adjacent Industrial R&D Nodes:** Identify the specific corporate metrology or failure-analysis teams (typically 15 to 40 companies globally in quantum computing, specialty semiconductors, or bioprocessing) transitioning from academic collaborations to in-house pilot lines.

## Turning a €4M Annual SOM into an Undeniable Grant Case

Many researchers worry that if they state the truth—for example, that the realistic global demand for their niche scientific instrument is 30 units per year at [a properly structured €140,000 price point]({filename}/how-to-price-niche-scientific-instrument.md) (€4.2M annual revenue)—grant committees will deem the project "too small."

The opposite is true when you frame the numbers correctly. Non-dilutive funding bodies like [NWO Take-off]({filename}/nwo-takeoff-budget.md) and European valorisation programs are specifically mandated to fund [additionality in deep tech grants]({filename}/proving-additionality-deep-tech-grants.md): high-impact scientific tools that unlock breakthroughs across dozens of downstream laboratories and industries, even when conventional venture capital refuses to touch them.

When you present a table naming the exact 165 target laboratories worldwide, backed by three signed Letters of Intent (LOIs) from tier-1 PIs stating they have budgeted €140,000 for your system once it passes [CE-marking requirements]({filename}/ce-marking-requirements.md) and ships with a stable Python/PyQt control stack, you stand out from 95% of applicants. Furthermore, showing how you will deliver Serial #001 profitably by leveraging an 80/20 [Science Breadboard]({filename}/breadboard.md) architecture and an [asset carve-out]({filename}/asset-carveout-vs-spinoff.md) execution model proves to reviewers that public grant capital will go directly into hard engineering rather than executive overhead.
