Summary: Why top-down, artificially inflated Total Addressable Market (TAM) numbers disqualify deep tech hardware grants in front of expert juries, and how to build a defensible bottom-up TAM, SAM, and SOM model rooted in low-volume unit economics.

When an academic founder or hardware engineer engages an [outsourced grant-writing agency]({filename}/outsourcing-deep-tech-grants.md) to tackle an [EIC Accelerator]({filename}/eic-accelerator-deep-tech.md) or Eurostars application, the draft that comes back often looks spectacular. The narrative is polished, the formatting is compliant, and the market potential is astronomical. 

However, hidden within this polished document is often a fatal flaw that will immediately disqualify the project in front of an expert jury: a generic, artificially inflated market analysis.

## The Allure of the Inflated TAM

In the ["Impact" or commercialization section]({filename}/write-horizon-impact.md) of the proposal—one of the [four universal pillars of a winning deep tech grant]({filename}/anatomy-of-a-winning-deep-tech-grant.md)—you will frequently find a claim that your niche scientific instrument is targeting a "€50 Billion Global Semiconductor Market." This massive number represents the Total Addressable Market (TAM). 

Outsourced grant writers often default to this generic approach because [traditional venture capital demands massive markets]({filename}/vc-funding-mismatch.md), and a top-down market analysis is much faster to draft. They copy an industry-wide revenue total from an analyst report—which includes products and buyer types entirely unrelated to your technology—and apply it to your startup. 

## Why Top-Down Fails the "Jury Test"

Expert reviewers and EIC Jury members (who are often seasoned industry operators and investors) see right through this facade. If you are developing a highly specialized optical metrology tool and claim a multi-billion dollar TAM, the jury will assume you do not know who your actual buyer is. 

Top-down analysis fails because it ignores segmentation and unit economics. The EIC Accelerator specifically evaluates whether your market and financial projections are built bottom-up from defendable assumptions, rather than relying on one huge, unsubstantiated number from a generic report. You must be able to explain every major figure line by line. 

## The Deep Tech Hardware Reality

For physical deep tech, the reality is that the market is often [niche, low-volume, and high-margin]({filename}/low-volume-monetization.md). You might only [sell 30 units a year]({filename}/pricing-niche-hardware.md) to [specialized research institutes or advanced fabs]({filename}/selling-to-fabs-vs-labs.md). At a premium price point, that creates a highly profitable, defensible business—but it is completely obscured by an inflated, generic TAM designed to mimic [software hyper-growth]({filename}/saas-playbooks-kill-hardware.md).

## How to Build a Bottom-Up Market Analysis

To convince expert reviewers, you must abandon the generic industry reports and build a bottom-up model based on actual revenue potential and customer buying behavior. This requires applying the TAM, SAM, and SOM framework with extreme precision:

*   **TAM (Total Addressable Market):** Start by defining the sellable unit (e.g., per device or per clinic) rather than an industry total. If there are exactly 500 specific R&D labs globally that suffer from the exact bottleneck your hardware solves, and your unit costs €200,000, your true TAM is €100M. 
*   **SAM (Serviceable Available Market):** Filter the TAM by practical business limitations. Reduce the total market by identifying the specific audience your product is built for, considering factors like geographic operating areas, [regulatory clearance (CE-marking)]({filename}/ce-marking-requirements.md), and pricing fit.
*   **SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market):** Estimate the percentage of the SAM your startup can realistically capture in the short term based on your sales capacity, production limits, and onboarding constraints. If your assembly team can only deliver 20 units a quarter, your financial model will break if your market sizing assumes you will sell 100.

## Validating the Numbers

A credible bottom-up market sizing model relies on hard data, not hope. 

*   **Lead with the Buyer:** Show exactly who pays, why they buy now, and the specific pain point you target.
*   **Secure Letters of Intent (LOIs):** Proving that early customers are willing to pay your stated unit price validates your pricing assumptions (see our guides on [securing your first 5 customers with meaningful LOIs]({filename}/securing-first-5-customers.md) and [gathering early industry letters of support]({filename}/letters_support.md)).
*   **Show Capacity Constraints:** Make sure your sales, marketing, and [low-volume supply chain capabilities]({filename}/supply-chain-low-volume.md) realistically match your projected SOM.

## Owning Your Economics

By embracing a bottom-up market analysis, you are telling the evaluators the truth about your technology. You do not need a €50 billion TAM to build a successful deep tech hardware company. A well-defended, highly targeted market analysis proves that you understand your customers, you respect the physics of [low-volume manufacturing]({filename}/low-volume-manufacturing.md), and you possess the operational maturity to turn [non-dilutive public grant money]({filename}/mapping-non-dilutive-landscape-deep-tech.md) into a [sustainable, capital-efficient business]({filename}/capital-efficient-hardware-startups.md).
