For Technology Transfer Offices (TTOs), managing the complex web of intellectual property and maximizing its commercial potential often requires strategic asset carve-outs. When extracting specific hardware assets from larger university portfolios, structuring the accompanying royalty agreements is a delicate balancing act. TTOs must ensure fair compensation for the institution and its inventors while providing the spin-out entity with enough financial breathing room to attract venture capital and reach commercial viability.

### The Complexity of Hardware Carve-Outs

Unlike software, hardware commercialization is inherently capital-intensive and time-consuming. The journey from a bench prototype to a manufacturable product involves significant engineering risk, supply chain development, and rigorous testing. Therefore, when a TTO structures a royalty agreement for a hardware asset carve-out, applying a standard software-style revenue share is often a recipe for failure.

Hardware companies typically operate on thinner gross margins than SaaS businesses, particularly in their early stages. Imposing a heavy royalty burden top-line revenue can severely hamper a hardware startup's ability to achieve profitability and scale. ### Structuring Strategic Royalties

To foster successful hardware carve-outs, TTOs should consider several advanced royalty structures:

#### 1. Tiered Royalty Rates Based on Volume or Revenue
Rather than a flat percentage, tiered royalties adjust the rate as the spin-out hits specific commercial milestones. In the initial phases, when margins are tight and production volumes are low, a nominal royalty rate (or even a royalty holiday) allows the company to reinvest capital into manufacturing and scale. As sales volumes increase and economies of scale improve margins, the royalty rate can step up. This aligns the TTO’s upside with the startup's financial maturity.

#### 2. Margin-Based Royalties
While revenue-based royalties are standard, hardware startups might benefit from agreements tied to gross margin. This approach acknowledges the high Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) in hardware manufacturing. By taking a percentage of the gross margin rather than top-line revenue, the TTO shares in the production risk. If manufacturing costs spike unexpectedly, the startup isn't penalized by an inflexible revenue royalty.

#### 3. Minimum Annual Royalties (MARs) with Catch-Up Provisions
MARs are essential for TTOs to ensure that the licensee is actively commercializing the technology. However, strict MARs can be fatal for early-stage hardware companies facing unexpected delays in product development or certification. A strategic approach involves structuring MARs that scale over time, incorporating "catch-up" provisions. If a company misses a MAR one year due to a specific technical delay, they can make it up in subsequent years without defaulting on the license.

#### 4. Equity-Royalty Trade-offs
Often, the most effective structure involves a hybrid approach. TTOs can negotiate a larger upfront equity stake in the spin-out in exchange for lower ongoing royalty rates. This is particularly attractive to venture capitalists, who prefer to see capital retained within the company to fund growth rather than siphoned off to licensors. A strong equity position aligns the TTO entirely with the long-term enterprise value of the hardware carve-out.

### Navigating Sublicensing Revenues
In hardware, the spin-out may eventually sublicense the core technology to larger manufacturers or integrators. Structuring how sublicensing revenue is shared is critical. Typically, TTOs take a higher percentage of non-royalty sublicensing income (like upfront fees) in the early years, which decreases as the spin-out adds value to the technology before sublicensing it.

### Conclusion

Designing royalty structures for hardware asset carve-outs requires TTOs to think like venture investors rather than pure licensors. By employing tiered, margin-based, or equity-weighted structures, TTOs can provide the financial flexibility these deep-tech spin-outs desperately need. This strategic foresight not only increases the probability of the startup's survival but ultimately maximizes the long-term financial return for the research institution.
