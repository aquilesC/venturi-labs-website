When a deep tech startup finally transitions a scientific instrument from a working prototype to a commercial product, the most pressing question becomes: How do we get this into the hands of customers? For highly specialized, low-volume lab tools, the choice between building a direct sales force and leveraging third-party distributors is one of the most consequential strategic decisions a founder will make.

Choosing the wrong channel can lead to burned capital, stagnant growth, and ultimately falling into the classic [startup trap]({filename}startup_trap.md) of building a great product that no one buys.

### The Appeal and Peril of Distributors

On paper, distributors look like a startup's dream. They already have relationships with your target customers. They understand the procurement processes of major universities and pharmaceutical companies. They have boots on the ground in geographies you couldn't possibly cover with a small team. 

However, distributors are not a magic bullet; they are a fulfillment channel. 

The harsh reality is that distributors will rarely evangelize a complex, unproven technology. Distributor sales reps carry catalogs with thousands of products. They are incentivized to sell what is easy, established, and frequently restocked (like consumables and standard benchtop equipment). 

If your instrument requires missionary selling—educating the market, explaining a novel mechanism of action, or proving ROI to a skeptical PI—a distributor rep will quickly lose interest. They will happily take the order if a customer asks for your product by name, but they will not create the demand. Furthermore, distributors typically demand a 20% to 40% margin, which can cripple the economics of a low-volume hardware business.

### The Case for Direct Sales

For novel scientific instruments, direct sales is almost always mandatory in the early years. 

A direct sales approach allows you to control the narrative. Your founders and early sales engineers possess the deep technical expertise required to go toe-to-toe with domain experts, answer complex scientific questions, and run compelling demonstrations. 

More importantly, direct sales creates a tight feedback loop. When a direct rep loses a deal, they know exactly why—was it missing a crucial feature, was the software UI confusing, or was the price point misaligned with the grant structure? This market intelligence is vital for refining the product. A distributor will simply report that the product "didn't sell."

The drawback of direct sales is the high fixed cost. Hiring experienced technical sales professionals who understand deep tech is expensive, and travel costs for on-site demos quickly add up. 

### A Hybrid, Phased Approach

The most successful commercialization strategies for niche hardware rarely rely on a single channel. Instead, they employ a phased approach that evolves as the product matures.

**Phase 1: Founder-Led Direct Sales (Units 1-20)**
In the beginning, the founders must sell. No one else has the passion or the deep technical knowledge to secure the first critical early adopters. The goal here is not just revenue, but learning. You are refining the pitch, discovering the true objections, and proving that the market cares.

**Phase 2: Direct Technical Sales Team (Units 21-100)**
Once the value proposition is validated and the sales cycle is somewhat understood, you hire a small team of direct sales engineers. They handle your primary domestic market (e.g., North America). They continue the missionary work, deeply supported by the engineering and application teams.

**Phase 3: Strategic Distributors for Geography and Scale (Units 100+)**
Only when you have a proven, repeatable sales playbook, robust marketing collateral, and a product that requires minimal hand-holding should you engage distributors. Even then, use them strategically. Deploy distributors to crack complex international markets (like Japan or Europe) where local language and entrenched relationships are mandatory. Provide them with highly qualified leads generated by your own marketing efforts, rather than expecting them to prospect from scratch.

### Conclusion

For low-volume, high-complexity lab tools, you cannot outsource the burden of market creation. Startups must embrace the difficult work of direct sales to establish a foothold and understand their customers. Distributors are a powerful tool for scaling an established product, but relying on them to launch a novel technology is a recipe for commercial failure.
